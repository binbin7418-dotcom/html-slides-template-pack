COVER STORY · THE SURVIVAL OF INDEPENDENT CAFÉS

The 4 a.m. roaster is more honest than any business plan

Halfway Café has been open for seven years on a side street in the old town: thirty-four square metres, nine seats. Every night after closing, the owner, Marta Ibarra, roasts the next day's beans herself. It is not romance, she says — it is arithmetic. Hire a roaster, and the shop has no profit left; roast it herself, and the margin grows by eleven points.

She is not unusual. Of the sixty-one independent cafés we visited, forty-three have an owner who personally covers at least two jobs — roasting, buying, bookkeeping, and the bar, often all in the same person.

"People don't come for the caffeine. They come because this place is still here."
— MARTA IBARRA, OWNER, HALFWAY CAFÉ

The real moat of the small shop is not the product. It is the five-hundred-metre circle of regulars: she knows their orders, remembers whose kid has exams this spring. No central kitchen can copy that — and it is why landlords renew her lease when the rent goes up.

WHY SMALL WINS SLOWLY

Scale was supposed to win. It won something else.

The chains did not lose because their coffee got worse. They lost because the thing they optimised — a identical cup, anywhere — stopped being scarce. A traveller can get that cup at the airport. What she cannot get at the airport is a room where somebody knows her name, and small shops turned out to be the only businesses still selling it.

There is a catch, and this issue does not hide it: the model only works while the owner's labour stays cheap — usually her own. The shops in our sample that failed had almost always tried to hire their way out of the back room too early. Small wins slowly, or it does not win at all.
